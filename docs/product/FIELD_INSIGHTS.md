# Field insights: one farmer interview (2026-09-29)

**Source.** Shafin Bhai phoned his uncle (mama), a farmer in **Parhail village, Dinajpur**,
and sent the team four voice-note transcripts (Messenger screenshots 1–4). This is **one
farmer's account**: it is evidence of how the problem feels, not survey data. Quote it as
"a farmer in Dinajpur told us", never as a statistic.

## What he said, in order

1. **Where decisions come from.** Which crop in which season, and which fertiliser, come from
   what fathers and grandfathers did, plus what the shopkeeper (দোকানদার) says.
2. **Getting the fertiliser wrong hurts.** Fertiliser applied at the wrong time or in the wrong
   amount has caused real losses, mostly in **boro (ইরি) season**, when many kinds of
   fertiliser are needed.
3. **Asking someone is hard.** *(Not for the video; the team rule is no hotline mention.)*
   - The hotline mostly leaves him waiting. When someone answers, he feels he knows more
     than they do.
   - The upazila agriculture office is far away. He has gone once in his life, and the trip
     cost more than it helped.
4. **The biggest problem: "what disease is this?"**
   - He cannot tell what is wrong with the crop, and he cannot describe it well enough for the
     shopkeeper to understand.
   - So he does not know which fertiliser or medicine will fix it.
5. **Confusion.**
   - Every shopkeeper says something different, and every neighbour says something different.
   - Everyone is confused, and the agriculture office is the last resort. "A gap exists."
6. **Costs up, yield down.**
   - Hybrids came 15–20 years ago and production rose.
   - Over the last ten years production has fallen while fertiliser and other costs rose.
   - His own example: "50 মণ last year, 30 মণ this year" (his figures; land size not stated).
7. **Weather warnings.** A warning before a storm helps on a small plot. Big paddy fields
   cannot be covered, so a bare "storm coming" gives no benefit there. **A warning is only
   useful when it tells him what to do or not do** (hold the urea, delay the spray).
8. **Would he use it?** "If there were an app where you ask and get a good answer directly:
   yes, a good initiative, if anything can be known easily."
9. **Phones (Shafin Bhai's note).** In this village almost everyone already has a smartphone.
   So the button phone need not be the focus.

## What it means for us

- **The core pain is confusion and no one to trust, not a lack of information.**
  - He hears too many answers: seller, neighbour, tradition. None is tied to his crop, his
    land or today's weather, and none can be checked.
  - Our job is to be **the one answer he can trust**, clear enough to act on and to show the
    shopkeeper.
- **"Describe the problem" is the hard step.** He struggles to explain the symptom.
  - So the service has to *ask* him, step by step: crop, days since planting, which part,
    what it looks like.
  - That is what our crop-problem menu and voice flow do.
  - A photo diagnosis (already prototyped in `leaf_diagnosis.py`) helps smartphone users.
- **Amounts and timing are where money is lost.** Our strongest proof point is the dose worked
  out for *his* land in *his* units (bigha), and the "hold the fertiliser, rain is coming" SMS.
- **Rising costs and falling yields** make the finance side matter: the cost before he spends
  it, and the expected return.
- **Weather alerts must be actions, not news.** "Heavy rain on the 12th: put the urea on after
  it" beats "a storm is coming".
- **Channel emphasis shifts.**
  - Voice and a simple smartphone flow come first, because smartphones are common in his
    village.
  - Button phone and SMS stay as the way in for anyone without one.
  - Keep both claims honest: GSMA 2024 says 26% of rural *adults own* a smartphone; many
    households share one.

## For the video (without breaking the team's rules)

The scene that writes itself: **a diseased patch of paddy, three people, three different
answers** (the seller, a neighbour, "my father always used…"). The farmer is confused. He
asks the service and gets one clear answer: what it is, how much for his 2 bigha, and when. He
shows the SMS at the shop. Then the SMS to hold the urea before the rain. No hotline, no office
trip, no drama. The feeling is relief: "এবার বুঝলাম।"
