IDEA TITLE

Urbora (উর্বরা): Bangla crop advice for each farmer's own land, even on a button phone


PROBLEM STATEMENT (273/300 words)

Fertile soil is Bangladesh's biggest gift, but it is wearing thin. A farmer in Dinajpur told us his harvest fell from 50 maund last year to 30 this year, while fertiliser kept getting dearer.

He has plenty of advice, but no answer he can trust. When the crop falls sick, a pest, a disease and a soil shortage can look alike, and the signs are hard to put into words. So he asks around. The shopkeeper suggests one thing, a neighbour another, and his father always used something else. The answers differ, and he has no way to check which one is right. In a survey of 120 rice farmers, 69% got their pesticide advice from dealers (Rahaman et al., 2018).

Nationwide, the soil is under strain. The Soil Resource Development Institute's map puts 78% of the land below 1% organic matter (Prothom Alo, July 2026). A World Bank report this year found that 95% of farmers use fertiliser in the wrong balance, and nine in ten give too little sulphur. Getting the balance right could lift boro yields by a third (World Bank, 2026). Our farmer says wrong amounts and timing have cost him most in boro.

The knowledge exists, in guides and with agriculture officers, but for him the upazila office is far away. He has been there once in his life. Farming apps could help, but they expect reading, typing and mobile data. Only 26% of rural people use a smartphone (GSMA, 2024), and about 28% of rural people aged seven and over are not literate (Census 2022). Farming still employs 45% of the country's workers (Labour Force Survey 2022).


PROPOSED SOLUTION (497/500 words)

Urbora (উর্বরা) brings expert crop advice to farmers on any phone, the way mobile banking brought the bank to people's hands. On a smartphone the farmer can talk, type or send a photo. Button phone users can call and speak Bangla, press keys, or text. Every answer also comes as an SMS to keep and show at the shop. A son or daughter can read it out.

It helps with three questions.

1. What is wrong with my crop, and what do I do? Urbora doesn't expect the farmer to name the problem. It asks, one step at a time: which crop, how many days since planting, which part of the plant, what it looks like, and how much of the field is hit. It then works out the likely cause: a pest, a disease, or a soil shortage such as sulphur or zinc in boro. The treatment comes from cited sources, with the amount worked out for the farmer's land in bigha and grams. It gives a dose only when one cause clearly fits, and every spray answer says to wear a mask and gloves. If the case is unclear or widespread, it tells the farmer to see the local agriculture officer, with the details in the SMS.

2. What should I do this week? Urbora remembers the farm: crop, sowing date, land size. So it knows the crop's stage and what is due next. When the forecast clashes with the plan, it says what to do: "Rain is forecast on your urea day. Put it on the next dry day."

3. What should I grow, and will it pay? Before a season, it ranks the crops that suit the land and weather, and shows the cost, yield and profit for the farmer's plot, with a dated plan and the right fertiliser for each stage.

Say Mahmud grows two bigha of boro. The centre shoots of some plants are drying. The shopkeeper suggests one spray; a neighbour thinks the soil needs potash. Mahmud calls Urbora from his button phone and answers a few questions. Urbora tells him it looks like stem borer, not a soil problem, and gives the dose for his land: 20 grams of the insecticide it names. He shows the SMS at the shop. Ten days later another SMS warns that rain is due on his urea day, so he waits for a dry one.

For inputs, Urbora lists nearby dealers by price, distance and stock; none can pay to rank higher. Farmers can also list their harvest for buyers. A small fee on sales, the same for every dealer and product, aims to keep the advice free.

The fee can't change the advice. The diagnosis and dose come from fixed rules before any shop is mentioned, and the fee is the same whatever the farmer buys. Other numbers come from named sources such as weather forecasts and price statistics. When the data isn't there, Urbora says "তথ্য যথেষ্ট নয়" (not enough information) rather than guess.


HOW DOES AI ENABLE YOUR SOLUTION? (294/300 words)

Without AI, a button-phone user would face long keypad menus; with it, they can just say what they see. AI does four jobs.

1. On a phone call, AI turns spoken Bangla into text and reads the answer back in a natural voice, so a farmer who can't read can still use Urbora. We use Sarvam's speech model; on a public Bangla test it made about a quarter as many errors as a leading general model. We test it next on farmers' calls.

2. A farmer might say "এক বিঘা জমি, পানি জমে থাকে" (one bigha, water stands in it). A language model (OpenAI's GPT-4o) turns everyday words like these into farm details and asks only for what is missing.

3. On a smartphone, a photo model suggests likely problems from a leaf picture. It only knows potato well so far, so the questions always check its guess.

4. AI finds the right passages in our sources and explains the answer in plain Bangla, saying where it came from.

AI doesn't set the numbers or make the final call. The likely cause is matched by fixed rules built on cited sources. Crop ranking, doses, costs and dates come from tested calculations, and every number shown has a source.

Speech recognition can mishear a dialect, so key facts are read back for the farmer to confirm, and the keypad is always there instead of speaking. When no single cause clearly fits, Urbora gives no dose and tells the farmer to see the local agriculture officer. Farm details are saved only with the farmer's agreement, and farmers are told that outside AI services process their voice. Phone numbers sit in one table, used only to send replies; identity uses a salted hash, and logs show just the last three digits.


IMPACT POTENTIAL (295/300 words)

Urbora is built for smallholder farmers, including the ones a smartphone app would miss: people with basic phones, people who read with difficulty, older farmers who would rather talk than type, and women farmers who can ask from home. That covers the language, literacy and access barriers this category names.

A farmer buys the treatment the crop needs, in the amount worked out for their land. Fertiliser goes on at the right stage, on a dry day. The right dose instead of "a bit extra to be safe" means less chemical in the soil and water, and whoever sprays breathes in less. Before switching crops they see the likely cost and profit. They reach the shop with one clear answer instead of three opinions.

Because answers name the cause, farmers can learn to spot problems themselves, and the SMS history becomes the family's farm notebook.

Phone advice does change behaviour. A review in Science (Fabregas, Kremer and Schilbach, 2019) found that mobile agricultural information raised yields by 4% and the odds of adopting recommended inputs by 22%, and that even cheap text and voice messages can change what farmers do.

Crop-problem reports are also counted by district and crop, with no identity attached. With enough farmers, those counts can warn agricultural authorities and researchers of pest outbreaks early.

Every handset can make a call, send an SMS and open a USSD menu. Bangladesh has 189.8 million mobile connections (BTRC, June 2026) and 16.9 million farm holdings (Agriculture Census 2019). One system answers many farmers at once, without a person per call.

In the pilot of about 500 farmers, we will compare spending, yield and harvest price with villages chosen at random to join later, and an agronomist will check a sample of diagnoses every month.


FEASIBILITY (EXECUTION PLAN, 2–3 YEARS) (295/300 words)

We built the first version at the IUT 12th ICT Fest Agentic AI Hackathon in July 2026 and have kept working on it since. The prototype covers five crops: aman and boro rice, potato, mustard and lentil. SMS works on real SIM cards. Voice calls and the USSD keypad menu run on our simulator, and real calls are in testing.

Year 1, late 2026 to 2027: pilot. We will record consenting farmers in several regions so Urbora learns local Bangla. An agronomist will review every crop-problem rule and season plan, and we will base each dose on BRRI and DAE guides where they exist. Then about 500 farmers in two upazilas, recruited through a local NGO or farmer group, use it for one boro season on Grameenphone's voice line, USSD short code and SMS, with verified input dealers nearby.

Year 2, 2027 to 2028: more crops and vegetables, cited seed and pesticide prices, and more agronomist-reviewed guidance. Harvest selling opens to verified buyers, with fees set from the pilot's real costs.

Year 3, 2028 to 2029: expand district by district and offer the service to agribusinesses, cooperatives and development programmes. We move ahead only when farmers keep coming back, agronomists rate the answers safe, and the fees cover costs.

What we need: a voice line, USSD code and SMS that are free for farmers; cloud and AI credits; agronomist time; a field partner; and the upazila agriculture office's support.

Dialects are the first risk, so we record regional voices, read key facts back and keep the keypad. Wrong advice is the worst: no dose when Urbora is unsure, every answer logged, and farmers texted if a mistake is fixed. For slow uptake, we use phone habits farmers already have and recruit through people they trust.


DIFFERENTIATION (191/200 words)

Asking the shopkeeper or a neighbour is free and close, and they want to help. But their answers differ, and the farmer can't tell which is right. Instead of one more opinion, Urbora gives a sourced answer based on this farmer's crop, its stage, the size of the land and this week's weather. Nobody can pay it to recommend anything.

Most farm apps need a smartphone and data. Photo apps judge from a picture, yet pests, diseases and soil shortages can look alike, and a general chatbot can state a wrong dose with total confidence. Urbora works on any phone. Its questions help tell those causes apart, and every amount is calculated from published data, not made up by the model. Cases it can't settle are referred to the local agriculture officer, with the details written down.

What is new: on a button phone, even a farmer who can't read is guided by voice or keypad to a likely cause. Then they get an exact dose for their land with its source, or none when unsure, an SMS to show at the shop, and weather warnings that say what to do.
